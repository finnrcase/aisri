# Market Recommendation Framework

This document classifies the eight markets into three strategic categories
and defines what a business development team should do with each.

The categories are not simply high-score / mid-score / low-score buckets.
They reflect the combination of opportunity score, the nature of the
opportunity (near-term vs. medium-term, demand-led vs. developer-led),
and the specific constraints that determine what action is appropriate.

---

## Category A — Priority Markets
*Pursue actively. Allocate BD and development resources now.*

**Criteria:** High composite score AND actionable near-term development
opportunity AND strategic alignment with a clean energy developer's model.

---

### Pacific Northwest (WA) — Score: ~66 | Rank 1

**Strategic rationale:**
The Pacific Northwest's hydro-dominant clean baseload, best-in-dataset
grid reliability, and established hyperscale tenant base represent the
closest existing analog to the integrated clean energy + compute model
that differentiates a developer like Intersect from generic data center
operators. The market has proven demand, clean power economics that work
today, and a permitting environment that is faster than the coastal
alternatives.

**Recommended near-term actions:**
- Begin site identification and landowner outreach in the Columbia River
  corridor and the Puget Sound industrial belt before competition for sites
  intensifies.
- Build utility relationships with Puget Sound Energy, Pacific Power, and
  the Bonneville Power Administration. Regulated utilities in this region
  have experience with large industrial loads and are the relevant counterparties
  for interconnection and power supply discussions.
- Engage with Microsoft and Amazon corporate sustainability teams on potential
  long-term power agreements. Both companies have made 24/7 clean power
  commitments and have demonstrated willingness to execute PPAs for adjacent
  or co-located generation.

**Key risk to monitor:**
Long-run hydro output under drought scenarios. Commission a climate-adjusted
resource adequacy study before committing to project financing.

---

### ERCOT Texas — Score: ~64 | Rank 2

**Strategic rationale:**
Texas offers the strongest execution environment in the country for large
energy infrastructure. The shortest interconnection queue, fastest permitting,
deepest PPA market, and largest developable renewable pipeline make it the
market where a developer can move from site selection to first power delivery
in the shortest time. The demand side is growing rapidly. The strategic fit
for a co-location model is strong.

**Recommended near-term actions:**
- Prioritize site evaluation in the Dallas-Fort Worth load zone and the
  Houston Ship Channel corridor, where transmission is available and
  the hyperscale tenant base is concentrating. West Texas offers better
  renewable resources but requires transmission solutions that add cost
  and timeline.
- Engage ERCOT's interconnection process early. The queue is the fastest
  in the dataset, but it is filling. Filing position matters.
- Develop a water management thesis before committing to sites. Identify
  sites with municipal water access or viable closed-loop cooling designs.

**Key risk to monitor:**
Grid reliability under extreme weather events. Conduct due diligence on
weatherization improvements since 2021 and their adequacy under plausible
stress scenarios. This will be a hyperscale tenant's first question.

## Category B — Watchlist Markets
*Monitor closely. Build relationships and optionality. Do not yet allocate
significant development capital.*

**Criteria:** Real medium-term opportunity, but one or more material
constraints limit near-term actionability. Conditions are likely to improve
over a 2–4 year horizon with identifiable catalysts.

---

### MISO Iowa — Score: ~60 | Rank 3

**Why it is a watchlist rather than a priority:**
The energy economics are excellent — the best in this dataset on a
cost-per-megawatt-hour basis. But the absence of concentrated hyperscale
demand means that entering this market requires bringing a customer thesis,
not following one. That is a longer and less certain path than entering
a market where demand already exists.

**What would move Iowa to Priority status:**
- A hyperscale tenant announcing a new data center campus in Iowa or the
  greater MISO region. Google's existing Iowa presence is a proof of concept;
  a second major tenant would validate the market.
- Fiber backbone expansion that reduces the connectivity gap with the
  established data center markets.

**Recommended near-term actions:**
- Identify and option attractive sites in advance of demand materializing.
  Land costs are low now. They will not stay low if a major hyperscaler
  commits to the market.
- Engage Iowa Economic Development Authority. The state has been active in
  attracting data center investment with tax incentives and has prior
  experience with Google's development process.

---

### Southeast Georgia — Score: ~53 | Rank 4

**Why it is a watchlist rather than a priority:**
Georgia's demand signal is improving with recent Microsoft and Google
announcements, but it has not yet reached the scale that creates self-
reinforcing market momentum. The renewable resource quality is modest,
which means a developer's clean energy story in this market depends more
heavily on PPA procurement than on co-located generation.

**What would move Georgia to Priority status:**
- Two to three additional hyperscale announcements in metropolitan Atlanta,
  establishing critical mass in the market.
- Georgia Power completing additional clean energy procurement under its
  integrated resource plan, improving the grid's clean energy penetration
  and the availability of clean power contracts.

**Recommended near-term actions:**
- Track Georgia Power's IRP process and any renewable energy tariff
  developments. The regulated utility structure means state-level policy
  is the key lever for clean energy access in this market.
- Build relationships with the Metro Atlanta Chamber and Georgia Department
  of Economic Development, which are the primary channels for inbound
  tech-sector expansion inquiries.

## Category C — High-Potential but Constrained
*Understand the constraints in detail. Do not pursue as a near-term development
opportunity. Revisit if specific constraints are resolved.*

**Criteria:** Strong on one or two dimensions but has a specific, material
constraint that makes near-term development either very difficult, very
expensive, or both.

---

### SPP Kansas — Score: ~51 | Rank 6

**The potential:** Highest wind capacity factors in the dataset, lowest land
costs, fast interconnection, and essentially no competition from other developers
yet. If the demand constraint is solved, the economics of a Kansas development
would be compelling.

**The constraint:** There is no existing hyperscale demand base and limited
fiber connectivity to major population centers. The absence of demand is not
simply a matter of awareness — it reflects geography and the absence of the
digital infrastructure that underpins large-scale compute deployment. Solving
this requires either a specific tenant commitment before development or a
speculative bet on demand migrating to low-cost power markets.

**Recommended action:** Identify and option land at low cost. Land is cheap
and the optionality is valuable. But do not begin interconnection studies
or construction activity without an anchor tenant in hand.

---

### PJM Northern Virginia — Score: ~46 | Rank 7

**The potential:** The largest existing data center market in the world.
Demand is concentrated, proven, and growing. Every hyperscale operator has
a major presence here.

**The constraint:** This market is operationally mature to the point that
greenfield development is extremely difficult. Land is scarce and expensive.
The 42-month interconnection queue is the most severe in this dataset.
There is no space for co-located renewable generation. Power must be sourced
from distant resources through PPAs or RECS, which weakens the clean energy
value proposition.

**Recommended action:** Northern Virginia is a market for incumbents and
operators with existing positions. A new entrant without land or interconnection
rights already in place would be entering at the worst possible time from
a cost and timeline perspective. Monitor but do not pursue greenfield.

---

### CAISO California — Score: ~41 | Rank 8

**The potential:** The strongest state policy environment in the country.
The deepest and most mature corporate PPA market. Concentrated tech-sector
demand from companies with the strongest clean energy commitments.

**The constraint:** The California development environment is the most
difficult in this dataset on every execution metric. CEQA review adds
12–24 months to project timelines. The interconnection queue exceeds four
years. Land is the most expensive in the dataset. Water availability is
constrained and likely to get worse.

**Recommended action:** California is important for corporate PPA execution
and policy engagement — not for new greenfield infrastructure development.
A developer with California-domiciled customers should route that demand
to infrastructure being built in adjacent markets (Nevada, Arizona, Pacific
Northwest) where California clean energy policy can be satisfied through
contracted instruments without requiring California siting.

## What the recommendation framework does not answer

This classification addresses where to prioritize near-term BD effort and
site identification activity. It does not answer:

- **Site-specific feasibility.** Two sites in the same market can have
  very different interconnection, permitting, and water outcomes. Market-level
  classification is the starting point for a site search, not a substitute for it.

- **Project-level economics.** This framework does not include capital costs,
  construction timelines, revenue assumptions, or returns. A market that scores
  well here could still be economically unattractive if land acquisition or
  construction costs have increased since the data was collected.

- **Tenant-specific requirements.** Different hyperscale operators have
  different requirements for latency, power density, water usage, and clean
  energy matching. A market that works for one tenant's requirements may not
  work for another's.

The appropriate follow-on to this framework is a market entry feasibility
study for the two Priority markets, with site identification, preliminary
interconnection analysis, and landowner engagement as the deliverables.
